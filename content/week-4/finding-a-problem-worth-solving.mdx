# Finding a Problem Worth Solving

> Useful AI products do not start with "What can this tool do?" They start with "What problem does someone actually have?"

## Lesson Goal

By the end of this lesson, you will know how to look for a real problem before designing an AI product.

This matters because AI can make almost any idea sound exciting.

But a product is only useful when it helps a real person with a real task.

---

## Why Problem-First Thinking Matters

Many beginners start with the tool.

They say:

- I want to build something with AI
- I want to use a chatbot
- I want to add AI to an app
- I want to make a smart assistant

Those ideas can be fun, but they are still incomplete.

Before building, you need to know:

- who the product is for
- what problem they have
- why the problem matters
- what would make their life easier

When you start with the problem, AI becomes a helper.

When you start only with AI, the product can become a trick.

---

## Real Problem vs Random Idea

A random idea sounds interesting.

A real problem causes friction for someone.

### Random idea

"An AI app that writes things."

This is too broad. It does not say who needs it or what pain it solves.

### Real problem

"Students applying for internships struggle to turn their experience into a clear resume."

This is stronger because it includes:

- a specific user
- a specific task
- a clear pain point
- a reason AI could help

The goal is not to make the biggest idea.

The goal is to find a problem clear enough to solve simply.

---

## How To Observe Pain Points

A pain point is a moment where someone feels stuck, confused, slow, or frustrated.

You can find pain points by watching what people already do.

Ask:

- Where do people repeat the same task?
- Where do they ask for help?
- Where do they avoid starting?
- Where do they make the same mistake?
- Where do they spend too much time?

You do not need advanced research.

Start with everyday observation.

Look at school, work, family tasks, small businesses, content creation, job applications, planning, studying, or communication.

---

## Who Has The Problem?

A useful product has a clear user.

Not "everyone."

Start with one group.

Examples:

- first-year university students
- small business owners
- freelancers applying for work
- teachers preparing class activities
- creators writing weekly posts

The more specific the user, the easier it is to design something useful.

If you cannot describe the user, the problem is probably still too vague.

---

## How Often Does It Happen?

Some problems happen once.

Some happen every week.

Some happen every day.

Frequency matters because repeated problems are often better product opportunities.

Ask:

- Does this happen daily, weekly, monthly, or rarely?
- Does the user already spend time solving it?
- Would saving time here actually matter?

A small weekly problem can be more useful than a huge problem that only happens once.

---

## Why Does It Matter?

A problem matters when it has a cost.

The cost might be:

- wasted time
- stress
- confusion
- missed opportunities
- weak quality
- repeated mistakes

If the problem does not matter, people will not care about the product.

Your job is to understand the cost before choosing the AI feature.

---

## Example: Students Struggling With Resumes

Imagine a student applying for internships.

They may have school projects, volunteer work, part-time jobs, and skills.

But they struggle to describe those experiences clearly.

The problem is not "students need AI."

The real problem is:

> Students applying for internships struggle to turn their experience into strong resume bullet points.

This problem has a clear user, a clear task, and a real cost.

The cost is missed opportunities, weak applications, and stress.

Now AI can help with a specific job:

- ask for the student's experience
- suggest stronger bullet points
- improve clarity
- adapt the wording for a role

That is much better than simply saying "AI resume app."

---

## Mini Exercise

Choose one group of people you understand.

Write down:

1. Who are they?
2. What task do they struggle with?
3. How often does the problem happen?
4. Why does the problem matter?
5. What would a helpful result look like?

Keep the problem small and specific.

---

## Reflection Question

What is one real problem you have seen someone face that AI might help with, and why does that problem matter?
